Scaling Near-Optimal SFT–RL Annotation Budget Allocation from Small to Large LLMs

Splitting a fixed labeling budget between SFT and RL doesn't require finding the one perfect ratio. A wide region of splits comes within a few percent of it, the region grows as models get larger, and a cheap sweep on your smallest model tells you where it sits for your largest.

TL;DR

Instead of hunting for the single best SFT/RL split, we look at the ε-near-optimal region: every split that scores within a few percent of the peak. At a 2–10% tolerance this region is already wide. It gets wider as models grow, and it transfers from 1B proxies to 8B targets. A five-point sweep on the small model picks a ratio inside the big model's 5% region 94.3% of the time (1B to 8B, all tasks, Llama and Qwen 2.5). We see the same behavior in Llama 3, Qwen 2.5 and Qwen 3, on four tasks, with both DPO and GRPO.

Splitting a Labeling Budget Is Still Mostly Guesswork

Most LLM post-training happens in two stages. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on written demonstrations gives a pretrained model a reasonable starting policy. An RL stage then improves on it, using either preferences, as in DPORafailov et al., 2023. Direct Preference Optimization: Your language model is secretly a reward model. NeurIPS., or rewards, as in GRPOShao et al., 2024. DeepSeekMath: Pushing the limits of mathematical reasoning in open language models. arXiv:2402.03300.. Both stages need labeled data, and the labels cost far more than the training. Training compute costs roughly $2×10−5 to $3×10−4 per example, while one human annotation costs $0.5 to $1.0, three to five orders of magnitude more.

So anyone post-training on a budget has to decide: given a fixed annotation budget, how many labels should go to SFT and how many to RL? With too few SFT samples the starting policy is weak, and RL can't do much with it. Spend too much on SFT and little is left for preference or reward supervision. The best guidance so far (Raghavendra et al., 2025Raghavendra, Kang & Ritter, 2025. Balancing the budget: Understanding trade-offs between supervised and preference-based finetuning. ACL.) is a rough trend: SFT wins at small budgets and preference optimization catches up at larger ones. It doesn't tell you which ratio to pick, and it says nothing about whether a ratio chosen for one model size still works at another.

The question of practical interest is not the single best ratio but the set of ratios achieving near-optimal performance. From the paper, Section 2.1

You could run a grid search, but it gets expensive quickly. Each grid point is a full SFT-then-RL run, and every new ratio means training again from scratch. At 8B parameters and up, the sweep alone can eat a real share of your compute. Post-training also behaves unpredictably across scale. Algorithm rankings can flipLi, 2026. Do post-training algorithms actually differ? A controlled study across model scales uncovers scale-dependent ranking inversions. arXiv:2603.19335., metrics can jump discontinuously, and the best ratio for a small model may not be the best for a large one.

So we asked a different question. The exact optimum is noisy and hard to pin down. Instead of chasing it, we measure how wide the set of good-enough allocations is, how that set changes as models get bigger, and whether a set found cheaply on a 1B model still holds on an 8B one. In our experiments the set is wide, it widens with scale, and it usually carries over.

From a Sharp Optimum to a Forgiving Region

The method is simple: fix the budget, sweep the split, and look at the shape of performance around the peak rather than its exact location. Then compare that shape across model sizes.

Problem Setup

Take a pretrained model MN of size N and an annotation budget B. An allocation ratio r ∈ [0, 1] spends rB of the budget on SFT and (1 − r)B on the RL stage that follows. A cost ratio c = cSFT / cRL sets how much one demonstration costs relative to one RL label, so the spend buys rB / c SFT demos and (1 − r)B RL labels. At c = 1 this reduces to plain sample counting, the paper's default. The result is scored with a task metric P(N, r, B). Drag both controls below to see how the labels divide.

The Near-Optimal Region

The natural target is the point optimum r*(N, B) = argmaxr P(N, r, B). In practice, performance usually forms a broad plateau near the top, so a little noise can move the argmax a long way while the score barely changes. We work with four quantities instead:

r
Allocation Ratio

The SFT share of the budget, evaluated on the grid {0, .25, .5, .75, 1}. A denser nine-point grid finds the same regions.

ε
Near-Optimal Region

All ratios retaining at least a (1 − ε) fraction of peak. At ε = 0 it collapses to the point optimum.

w
Range Width

The largest ratio in the region minus the smallest, i.e. how much freedom you have. A count-based width gives the same qualitative trends.

T
Transfer Rate

The share of a small proxy's near-optimal ratios that are still near-optimal on a larger target. We define it in one direction on purpose: the question is whether the proxy's advice holds up on the target, not the reverse.

Definition 1 · ε-Near-Optimal Region
Rε(N, B) = { r ∈ [0, 1] : P(N, r, B) ≥ (1 − ε) · maxr′ P(N, r′, B) }
With ε = 5%, any ratio reaching 95% of peak performance qualifies.
Definition 2 · Cross-Scale Transfer Rate
Tε(Ns → Nt; B) = | Rε(Ns, B) ∩ Rε(Nt, B) | / | Rε(Ns, B) |
Measured from a small source Ns to a large target Nt at each budget, then aggregated.

The Proxy Recipe

In practice the recipe has three steps. The only added cost is one sweep on a small model:

Sweep the proxy

Train the smallest model in the target family at the five ratios {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} at the target budget.

Pick a tolerance

Decide how much peak performance you are willing to give up for flexibility. We recommend ε ∈ [5%, 10%], where transfer held for every family, task and budget we tested.

Train the big model at the midpoint

Take the proxy region [min Rε, max Rε] and use its midpoint, or the nearest grid point. Near-optimal ratios form a contiguous band, so the center still works if the target's band is shifted a little.

A Wide Plateau, Not a Sharp Peak

The first thing to check is whether the plateau is really there. For Llama3-8B and Qwen2.5-7B, the near-optimal region grows quickly as the tolerance increases. At ε = 10%, near-optimal ratios cover 55% to 75% of the allocation range for most tasks. Within that range, the exact split makes little difference.

Wider With Scale, and Transferable

If you fix the tolerance and increase N, the near-optimal region usually gets wider, across tasks and families. Under a strict tolerance, small models often have only a few good ratios, while larger models have many. Some of this is just arithmetic: a higher peak leaves more absolute room under a relative tolerance. With an absolute tolerance the widening still shows up for Qwen but is weaker for Llama, so we describe it as an effect that holds at a fixed tolerance.

For practitioners, transfer is the more useful result. At ε = 0, proxy-to-target transfer is sometimes high but unreliable, especially for Qwen 2.5. A modest tolerance raises it a lot, and consistently. Part of that gain is automatic, since a wider target region overlaps more of the proxy's. The hit-rate heatmaps also show that the ratios a small model hits most often usually overlap with, or sit inside, those of larger models. On-policy GRPO follows the same pattern, with a bit more variance.

Proxy vs. a Sensible Default

If the regions are this wide, why not just use r = 0.5? At a loose 10% tolerance, a centered default often does fall inside the region. It fails when you want a tighter tolerance or when the region is off-center, and off-center regions are common. The proxy handles both cases because it tells you where the region is.

The proxy sweep is also cheap. Five 1B runs take about 100 GPU-minutes, roughly the same as a single 8B run. The proxy plus one target run therefore costs about 200 GPU-minutes, compared with about 500 for sweeping the 8B model directly, and it lands in the 5%-near-optimal region more than 90% of the time on the first try.

When SFT Labels Cost More

Counting samples assumes a demonstration costs as much as a preference pair. In reality, written responses cost more. So we fix a dollar budget and set the cost ratio ρ = cSFT / cDPO ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10} (the c of Interactive A). The scaling and transfer trends barely change, but one new effect shows up: the more expensive SFT gets, the wider the near-optimal region tends to be. We also tried a GPU-time budget (ρ ≈ 0.5, since DPO takes about 2–3× the GPU time of SFT; tested on Llama math). There the region shrinks to essentially one ratio at small scale and only starts to widen at 8B. That ratio is the same at every size, though, so transfer is perfect.

Key Takeaway

Don't search for the best SFT–RL split on your big model. Sweep your smallest model, keep every ratio within 5–10% of its peak, and train the big one at the middle of that band.

Because the region found on a small model usually sits inside the one for a larger model, a cheap search on the small model can stand in for an expensive one on the big model. This holds for both off-policy DPO and on-policy GRPO. When SFT labels cost more than RL labels, the exact split matters even less, so practical concerns like logistics or data reuse can decide it.

What We Haven't Tested Yet

There are several limits on what these experiments can tell us:

In-Distribution Training

The training data matches each evaluation task. We don't yet know whether regions still transfer when a general-purpose data mix has to generalize to a held-out task, a setting that scales less predictably.

Scale Ceiling

Models go up to 14B and budgets to about 15k examples, close to the size of the public task datasets. Checking Llama 3 70B and Qwen 2.5 32B–72B is left for future work and may be beyond an academic compute budget.

Two RL Algorithms

We only test DPO and GRPO, not PPO, SimPO or others. That the two agree is encouraging, but it isn't proof for the rest.

A Simple Cost Model

Budget here means annotation cost only. Modeling annotation, compute and search cost together is left for future work. The main runs also use a single seed, and LoRA rather than full fine-tuning.

A natural next step is pipelines that aren't a single SFT→RL handoff, such as multi-stage or interleaved schedules, and settings where annotation costs are uncertain or change as you buy more labels. We expect the near-optimal-region view to suit both, since it already allows for the noise that makes a single best ratio unreliable.
